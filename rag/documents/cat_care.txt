ACTIVITY PATTERNS AND PLAY FOR CATS

Cats are crepuscular animals, naturally most active at dawn and dusk. This evolutionary pattern from their predatory ancestors means that interactive play sessions are most effective — and most enthusiastically received — in early morning and early evening. Scheduling play at these times aligns with the cat's internal rhythm and results in better engagement.

Adult cats sleep 12-16 hours per day, which is normal and not a sign of illness. Senior cats (11+ years) may sleep up to 20 hours. Sudden changes in sleep or activity patterns, especially a normally lively cat becoming unusually still or hidden, can signal pain, illness, or depression and warrant veterinary attention.

Indoor cats accumulate pent-up predatory energy with no natural outlet. Without adequate daily play, this energy emerges as midnight zoomies, inter-cat aggression, destructive scratching, or attention-seeking behavior. Two interactive play sessions of 10-15 minutes each per day — ideally at dawn and dusk — significantly reduce behavioral problems and improve overall wellbeing.

FEEDING AND NUTRITION FOR CATS

Most adult cats thrive on two meals daily, approximately 12 hours apart. Some obesity-prone cats benefit from three smaller portions. Automatic timed feeders maintain consistent schedules even when owners are away, which helps prevent the anxiety that irregular feeding causes in cats.

Cats are obligate carnivores and cannot synthesize taurine from plant sources. Taurine deficiency causes dilated cardiomyopathy and retinal degeneration leading to blindness. Use only cat-specific food formulations; never feed dog food long-term as it is nutritionally incomplete for feline needs.

Adequate hydration is critical for cats, particularly those eating dry kibble. Many cats prefer running water and will drink significantly more from a pet fountain than a still bowl. Insufficient water intake is a primary contributor to urinary tract disease and chronic kidney disease in cats. Placing water bowls away from food bowls encourages drinking, since cats instinctively avoid water near their prey (food source).

ENRICHMENT AND SCRATCHING FOR CATS

Cats need vertical space and hiding spots to feel secure. Cat trees, wall-mounted shelves, and cardboard boxes placed in different rooms provide environmental variety that reduces stress and prevents aggression. Window perches give indoor cats visual stimulation through bird and outdoor watching, which partially satisfies their predatory instincts without risk.

Scratching is a normal, necessary feline behavior serving three functions: claw maintenance, full-body stretching, and scent/visual territory marking. Provide at least one scratching post per cat plus one extra in high-traffic areas. Offer both vertical posts and horizontal scratchers, as individual cats have strong preferences. Posts must be tall enough for the cat to fully extend while scratching.

Rotating toys every 2-3 days maintains novelty and prevents habituation. Wand toys with feather or crinkle attachments best simulate prey movement and engage hunting instincts. Puzzle feeders that require cats to work for their kibble provide mental stimulation, slow eating (preventing vomiting in fast eaters), and enrich the daily routine.

GROOMING FOR CATS

Short-haired cats require brushing once or twice a week. Long-haired cats (Persians, Maine Coons, Norwegian Forest Cats) need daily brushing to prevent painful mats that can restrict movement and trap moisture against the skin. Regular brushing also significantly reduces hairball formation by removing loose fur before the cat ingests it during self-grooming.

Cats rarely need baths unless they have gotten into a harmful substance, have a skin condition requiring medicated baths, or are unable to self-groom due to obesity or injury. Check ears weekly for dark, crumbly debris that resembles coffee grounds, which is a common sign of ear mites. Nail trims every 2-3 weeks prevent overgrowth that can curl painfully into the paw pads.

HEALTH MONITORING FOR CATS

Monitor litter box habits daily. The litter box provides the earliest and most reliable health data for cats. Changes in frequency, volume, consistency, or odor of urine and feces are often the first indicators of illness. A cat repeatedly entering the litter box and producing little or no urine is a veterinary emergency, particularly in male cats, who are prone to life-threatening urinary blockages.

Annual veterinary exams are essential even for indoor cats. Indoor cats still require core vaccines and parasite prevention. Cats aged 11 and older benefit from biannual exams and routine bloodwork panels, which detect kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, and diabetes early — all conditions that are manageable if caught before significant organ damage occurs.
