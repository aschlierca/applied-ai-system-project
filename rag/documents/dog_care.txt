EXERCISE GUIDELINES FOR DOGS

Adult dogs (ages 1-7) require 30-60 minutes of vigorous exercise per day. High-energy breeds like Border Collies, Siberian Huskies, and Labrador Retrievers need 60-120 minutes daily. Low-energy breeds like Bulldogs and Basset Hounds are often satisfied with 20-30 minutes. Regular exercise prevents obesity, reduces destructive behavior, and supports cardiovascular health.

Puppies under 6 months should follow the "5-minute rule": no more than 5 minutes of structured exercise per month of age, given twice daily. Over-exercising puppies damages developing growth plates and can cause lifelong joint problems. Short play sessions and calm socialization are far better for young dogs than forced running or hiking.

Senior dogs (7+ years for large breeds, 10+ for small breeds) benefit from shorter, more frequent walks rather than one long outing. Watch for fatigue signals: excessive panting, slowing down, or reluctance to continue. Low-impact activities like gentle swimming are excellent for dogs with arthritis or hip dysplasia.

NUTRITION AND FEEDING SCHEDULES FOR DOGS

Most adult dogs should be fed twice daily — morning and evening — to maintain consistent energy levels and reduce the risk of bloat (gastric dilatation-volvulus), especially in deep-chested breeds like Great Danes and German Shepherds. Free-feeding (leaving food out all day) often leads to overeating and weight gain.

Puppies under 6 months need three to four small meals per day to support rapid growth and prevent hypoglycemia. Transition to twice-daily feeding between 6 and 12 months. Senior dogs may benefit from two slightly smaller meals to ease digestion and reduce the load on aging organs.

Fresh water must always be available. A dog's daily water intake should be approximately 1 ounce per pound of body weight. Sudden increases in thirst can indicate diabetes, kidney disease, or urinary tract infection, and warrant prompt veterinary evaluation.

GROOMING REQUIREMENTS FOR DOGS

Short-coated dogs (Beagles, Boxers, Dalmatians) need brushing once a week and bathing every 4-6 weeks. Long-coated breeds (Golden Retrievers, Afghan Hounds) require daily brushing to prevent painful mats and bathing every 4-8 weeks. Double-coated breeds (Siberian Huskies, German Shepherds) shed heavily during seasonal coat blows and need brushing 3-4 times weekly during those periods.

Nails should be trimmed every 3-4 weeks. Overgrown nails cause discomfort, alter gait, and can lead to long-term joint problems. A practical test: if you can hear the dog's nails clicking on hard floors, they are overdue for a trim. Check ears weekly for redness, unusual odor, or dark discharge, which may indicate infection or mites.

MENTAL ENRICHMENT FOR DOGS

Dogs require mental stimulation in addition to physical exercise. Without adequate enrichment, boredom manifests as destructive chewing, excessive barking, digging, or hyperactivity. Puzzle feeders, Kong toys stuffed with food, hide-and-seek games, and nose-work activities engage cognitive function and reduce anxiety.

Training sessions are most effective when kept to 5-10 minutes with positive reinforcement. Dogs have limited attention spans for focused obedience work. Two to three short training sessions per day produce better results than one long session. Always end on a successful repetition to build confidence and a positive association with training.

HEALTH MONITORING FOR DOGS

A brief daily health check catches problems early. Examine eyes (should be clear and bright, not cloudy or weepy), ears (no odor or redness), coat (shiny, no bald patches or excessive shedding outside normal seasons), and gum color (healthy gums are pink and moist). Pale, white, blue, or yellow gums are emergency signs requiring immediate veterinary care.

Track consistency in appetite, energy level, and bathroom habits. Any sudden change — prolonged lethargy, skipping two consecutive meals, vomiting more than twice in one day, bloody stool, or straining to urinate — warrants a call to your veterinarian within 24 hours.

MEDICATION ADMINISTRATION FOR DOGS

Administer medications with food unless the veterinarian has directed otherwise. Hiding tablets inside pill pockets, small amounts of peanut butter (xylitol-free), or a piece of soft cheese reduces resistance and prevents stomach upset. Never give medications prescribed for humans or other species without explicit veterinary guidance; many common human drugs (acetaminophen, ibuprofen, xylitol-containing products) are toxic to dogs.

Keep a written or digital medication log noting the date, time, and dose given. This prevents accidental double-dosing and provides your veterinarian with accurate compliance data. Never stop a prescribed course of medication abruptly without veterinary approval, as this can trigger relapse or withdrawal effects.
